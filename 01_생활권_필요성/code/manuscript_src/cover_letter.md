We are pleased to submit our manuscript, "Grids fill people, not places: Testing the necessity of living zones for walkable public services in Seoul", for consideration as a full-length article in *Cities*.

Cities around the world now plan daily life through living zones that are not their administrative districts, from neighbourhood units and life circles to the 15-minute city. Yet walking accessibility can be measured, and new facilities located, on fine grids. Why, then, are living zones needed at all? Our paper tests this question empirically. Using Seoul's 116 official living zones, a 100 m population grid, walking times on the street network and the public facilities the city actually added between 2020 and 2025, we compare grid-based placement with minimum standards set by districts, living zones and neighbourhoods.

The results give the living zone an empirical role. Grid-based placement serves people well but leaves 13–21 living zones, home to about a million residents, in which no resident can walk to all six public services the plan lists. Only a minimum standard set at the living-zone scale can fill all of them, as integer programming proves, at a cost of at least 1.3 percentage points of citywide access under a 5% minimum (0.6 under a 1% minimum in 2025). The same standard set by the larger district changes almost nothing, and set by the smaller administrative unit it could not be met with the facilities added.

We believe the paper fits the journal's aim of assessing urban planning policies and promoting their implementation. It speaks to planners and policymakers who design living-zone plans and 15-minute city programmes, and it offers a transferable test that any city with comparable data can apply. Seoul was chosen because it publishes all the data the test requires, including its official living-zone boundaries.

The manuscript has not been published and is not under consideration elsewhere. All authors have approved the submission. The main text contains about 9,000 words excluding references, with four tables, six figures and two appendices.
