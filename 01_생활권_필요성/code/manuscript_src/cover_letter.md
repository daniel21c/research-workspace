We are pleased to submit our manuscript, "Grids fill people, not places: Testing the necessity of living zones for walkable public services in Seoul", for consideration as a full-length article in *Cities*.

Cities around the world now plan daily life through living zones that are not their administrative districts, from neighbourhood units and life circles to the 15-minute city. Yet walking accessibility can be measured, and new facilities located, on fine grids. Why, then, are living zones needed at all? Our paper tests this question empirically. Using Seoul's 116 official living zones, a 100 m population grid, walking times on the street network and the public facilities the city actually added between 2020 and 2025, we compare grid-based placement with minimum standards set by districts, living zones and neighbourhoods.

The results give the living zone an empirical role. Grid-based placement serves people well but leaves 14–21 living zones in which no resident can walk to all six public services the plan promises. Only a minimum standard set at the living-zone scale removes almost all of them, at a cost of about two percentage points of citywide access. The same standard set by the larger district changes almost nothing, and set by the smaller neighbourhood it cannot be met with the facilities added.

We believe the paper fits the journal's aim of assessing urban planning policies and promoting their implementation. It speaks to planners and policymakers who design living-zone plans and 15-minute city programmes, and it offers a transferable test that any city with comparable data can apply. Seoul was chosen because it is one of the few cities that publishes all the data the test requires.

The manuscript has not been published and is not under consideration elsewhere. All authors have approved the submission. The main text contains about 7,700 words excluding references, with four tables, six figures and two appendices.
