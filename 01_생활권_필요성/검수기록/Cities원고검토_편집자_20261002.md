# Editorial screen: "Grids fill people, not places: Testing the necessity of living zones for walkable public services in Seoul"

Journal: *Cities* (Elsevier). Role: handling editor, desk screen and first editorial read. Date: 2 October 2026.
Material read: `manuscript.md` (full), Tables 1–4, Figs 1–6, Graphical Abstract, highlights, cover letter, title page, declarations.

## Verdict

**Send to review (probability that I would send it out: about 65%; desk-reject risk about 35%).** The paper asks a real planning-policy question that *Cities* readers care about (why plan through living zones and 15-minute neighbourhoods at all, when grids can measure and site facilities?), it is anchored in an adopted plan (Seoul's 2030 Living Zone Plan) with an explicit zone-level promise, it is well within the length limits (abstract 244 words; main text about 7,750 words), and the English is mostly clean and sparse. It is not a GIS showcase. The answer feeds a concrete rule for plans: measure on the grid, promise and monitor by zone. What pushes it towards the desk-reject pile is this. (i) Sections 3–4 and the appendix read like an operations-research paper: MCLP, MIP gaps, HiGHS, P = 2, Jaccard, "greedy is exact". The six-rule × three-unit × two-year design is hard to follow, and editors under the "highly experimental quantitative techniques" clause react to that texture. (ii) The core result is partly by construction. The "places" score counts zero-completion *official living zones*, and the winning rule constrains exactly those zones. The paper concedes this in one sentence (4.3.3) but does not neutralise it, and the first referee will make it the headline objection. (iii) The abstract and highlights merge two different grid rules into one claim, and they state "necessity" more strongly than a time-limited, single-city, 5%-threshold test supports. (iv) There is one city and no real replication argument, and the data-scarcity justification is contestable. None of these is fatal at the desk, but (ii) and (iii) need fixing *before* submission, because they determine whether referees read the paper as a policy argument or as a tautology wrapped in optimisation.

## Answers to the seven questions (summary)

1. **Desk-reject risk and framing.** The framing question ("cities plan by living zones; grids can do the job; are living zones needed?") is clear in the abstract and in paragraphs 1–4 of the Introduction, and it is one of the paper's real strengths. The title is less clear: "fill people" is not idiomatic, and "necessity" signals a logical proof rather than a policy test. Abstract and Introduction read as planning policy. Section 3.3, Section 4.4 and Appendix A read as technique. The balance tips towards technique in the middle third, and Fig. 2 and Table 4b use OR shorthand (MCLP, MIP, P=2) that a planner cannot decode.
2. **Contribution and international relevance.** The contribution is stated three times (Introduction, 2.3, 5.2). The most transferable insight is only implicit and should be made explicit. That insight is a scale rule: the unit must be fine enough that an average cannot hide an empty area, and coarse enough that the scarcest facility budget can reach every unit. The one-sentence "range of zone counts" analysis in 4.3.2 is the paper's best evidence for this, yet it has no figure and no numbers. The single-city choice is justified only by data availability, and that claim is weak: Germany (Zensus 2022) and the Netherlands (CBS) publish 100 m population grids, Japan publishes mesh data, and OSM covers facilities in many places. A policy justification would be stronger: Seoul is one of few cities with *official, mapped* living zones *and* a stated zone-level service promise *and* defined service domains.
3. **Structure.** The RQs are answered in order, but 4.2 (RQ2) already reports zone-based results (living-zone minimum, hybrid rule) and forward-references 4.3.3. RQ3 bundles two questions (unit, and boundaries). The "why grids leave zones empty" mechanism appears almost verbatim in 4.2.3 and 5.1. The contributions paragraph appears twice (end of 1 and 2.3). Section 4.4 is a long list that introduces results not defined in Methods (the "coordination gain", "greedy solutions"). The paper also never reports the most policy-relevant benchmark: what Seoul's *actual* 2020–2025 additions achieved compared with the counterfactual rules. Details are in the table below.
4. **Front matter.** Concrete rewrites are given below (title options, abstract fixes, six highlights of 82–85 characters, keyword swap, GA and cover-letter changes).
5. **Language.** British spelling is consistent in the main text: optimis-, neighbourhood, centre, programme, artefact, modelled, favour. The only "-ize/-or" forms are inside reference titles, which is correct. One unspaced em dash in 3.2 against en dashes elsewhere. The language problems are idiom and terminology rather than spelling: "fill people", "cut access", "report cards", "bottom-20", "completes the bundle", and "neighbourhood" used for three different things.
6. **Figures and tables.** Figure numbering is out of citation order (Fig. 5a is cited before Fig. 4). Table A.1 is cited but does not exist. Fig. 5's panel titles are clipped, and Fig. 1 lacks a locator inset. Rule names differ between the text, Table 4b and Fig. 6. Table 4a shows two of the seven facilities while the text cites the others. Some numbers in the text do not match the figures (youth centres 2025, senior facilities 2020, the "14–21" range).
7. **Overclaiming.** The main cases are listed as findings tagged [OC] in the table: the abstract's final sentence, "if, and only if", "only the living-zone scale", "better than any zone rule", "likely computational", "fills every place", and the generalised p ≤ 0.04.
